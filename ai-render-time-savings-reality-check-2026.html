Vendor Claim Audit

The 60–80% render time savings claim, audited.

Vendor blogs keep repeating the same line: AI rendering cuts architectural render times by 60–80%. We followed the figure back through the pages that state it and the surveys that exist. None of them shows where it was measured, and it counts less than it sounds.

Nora Sinclair
Nora Sinclair is ArchiGen's AI editorial persona, not a human architect or practitioner. Every piece published under her name is reviewed and approved by ArchiGen's human publisher. Full disclosure
9 min read · May 2026 · Evidence reviewed · Public sources only

Read enough AI rendering marketing in 2026 and one line keeps coming back: AI cuts architectural render time by 60–80%. It is stated as a settled fact, often near the top of the page, and usually with no footnote.

This piece is an audit of that sentence, not of any tool. ArchiGen has not run its own timing study, so there are no new measurements here. What we did instead was open the pages that repeat the figure, follow every citation they give, and read the surveys that actually exist. The question is simple: where does the number come from, and what is it counting?

The claim under audit
Vendor claim
AI Magicx · The KOW Company · Visoid · Cad Crowd · D5 Render

"AI is cutting architectural render times by 60-80%." (AI Magicx, 18 March 2026). Close variants: "Rendering times decrease by 60-80%" (The KOW Company), "reduce visualization times by over 80%" (Visoid), "Render times cut by up to 80%" (Cad Crowd).

Where the number appears

Here is what each page says, and what it offers as evidence.

Page Figure Evidence given
AI Magicx blog, March 2026 60–80% render time None cited
The KOW Company blog, January 2026 60–80% render time An unsourced example
Visoid blog (vendor), undated Over 80% visualization time None cited
Cad Crowd blog, September 2026 Up to 80%; 50–80% for AI denoising Tool names, no linked benchmark
D5 Render case studies (BIG, KPF) 80% video render time; up to 80% iteration time Named firms, no method described

Every one of these publishers has something to sell. Visoid, D5 Render and Rendervi sell rendering software. The KOW Company sells visualization services and lists its own AI products, including Holosnap, which its article names as one of the tools behind the speed-up. Cad Crowd runs a freelancer marketplace for visualization work. That does not make the figure false. It does mean none of these pages is an independent measurement.

Tracing it back

The AI Magicx guide opens with the 60–80% line and never returns to a source for it. Later in the same piece, under "reported results" by firm size, it lists "concept phase rendering time reduced by 70-80%" for mid-size firms and "per-render production time reduced 40-60%" for large firms. Its own cost table marks final presentation renders as "0% (use traditional)". Read closely, the article's own numbers are narrower than its headline.

The KOW Company's version is the only one that shows its working. It says a package of 10 residential images "that used to take 5-7 days to complete now takes just 24-48 hours" with AI tools, and concludes "this means a time reduction of 60-80%." The arithmetic is roughly right for the example. The example itself has no source: no firm, no project, no method, no definition of "complete."

Cad Crowd's table attributes "50–80% faster" AI denoising to "V-Ray, Arnold, DLSS benchmarks," but links to none of them. Denoising a path-traced render and generating an image from a SketchUp screenshot are different techniques, yet both get filed under "AI rendering."

Visoid's figure is the bluntest: "An iterative AI-powered workflow can reduce visualization times by over 80%." It is the opening line of a first-person deadline story, not a finding.

None of the pages that state 60–80% as a general fact points to a study that produced it.

The closest thing to evidence

The firmest-looking 80% figures come from D5 Render's own case studies, which aggregators such as Rendervi then summarise as "real firm use cases."

BIG. D5's case study is headed "Video Render Time Cut by 80%." The text credits "D5's real-time rendering capabilities" and says walkthroughs "which used to take 3–4 days, can now be completed in just a few hours." That is a real-time engine replacing an offline one for animation. It is not a generative AI result, and it is not about still images.

KPF. D5 says that with its AI Atmosphere Match feature, "tasks that used to take a week to produce multiple client drafts can now be completed in an afternoon, cutting iteration time by up to 80%." This one is an AI feature, but the metric is iteration time on drafts, stated as "up to," with no named person, baseline log or method.

Both are vendor-written success stories. They are useful as signals of where the gains show up: animation and early option-making. They are not a basis for a general percentage.

What the surveys actually say

The largest visualization-specific survey in these sources is the Chaos and Architizer AI in architecture report. Chaos says nearly 800 professionals took part in the online survey in November 2025; 70% work in architecture and 25% in interior design, and 50% are based in North America. The full report sits behind a download form; the published summaries are what is public.

Those summaries do not express savings as a percentage of render time. Chaos reports that 86% of current AI users report at least some time savings, and that for 59% this means at least 5 hours per week. On where the savings come from, 48% of AI users named concept design and ideation and 40% named image enhancement. Around 25% reported savings in material selection and asset generation. Chaos also notes that 40% of respondents were not yet using AI.

That is a self-reported, hours-per-week picture, concentrated in early design and post-production. It is consistent with AI saving architects real time. It does not show that any given render takes 60–80% less time, and the public summaries don't describe the question wording.

Broader industry surveys are more cautious on outcomes. Deltek's 2026 Clarity study, which drew on 896 A&E firms, found AI adoption rising from 53% to 70% year over year, but only 38% reported measurable positive business impact tied to AI, and 39% said it was too early to see benefits or they were still piloting. Bluebeam's 2026 outlook, based on over 1,000 AEC technology decision-makers, found that only 27% of firms use AI; among early adopters, nearly half reported reclaiming 500–1,000 hours, but on tasks like scheduling, planning and document analysis, not rendering.

What the figure measures, and what it leaves out

This section is our reading, not data.

Three technologies, one number. The pages above blend AI denoising inside a path tracer, real-time engines replacing offline renders, and generative image tools that restyle a model view. Each speeds up a different step. A percentage that borrows from all three describes none of them.

The denominator moves. "Render time" can mean machine compute, one artist's hours on one image, or the calendar time from model to approved deliverable. The examples behind the claim switch between these without saying so. A large cut in compute can coexist with a much smaller cut in the hours a project actually bills.

New work isn't counted. None of the claim pages accounts for the work generative tools add: writing and refining prompts, gathering references, keeping a set of views consistent with each other, and checking that the image still matches the design. We've written about counting those interventions separately. Whether that work is large or small for a given practice is exactly what an honest time study would need to show.

Deliverable type matters. Even AI Magicx's own table keeps final presentation renders on traditional pipelines. The evidence that exists, from the case studies to the Chaos survey, points to concept imagery, enhancement and animation, not every image a practice produces.

What to ask before believing it

If a vendor, a consultant or a blog quotes 60–80% at you, these questions sort a measurement from a slogan:

  1. Which task? Compute time, one still, a set of views, an animation, or the whole visualization deliverable?
  2. Against what baseline? Which renderer, which hardware, and which level of finish on both sides?
  3. Which kind of "AI"? Denoising, real-time rendering, or a generative tool working from a screenshot?
  4. Who measured, and how? Logged hours on a named project, a survey answer, or an estimate in a marketing page?
  5. What was added? Are prompting, reference gathering, consistency checks and extra review rounds inside the number or outside it?
  6. Was the output equivalent? Same views, same design accuracy, accepted by the same reviewer?

If the answers aren't available, treat the figure as a description of the best case on a narrow task.

Our view: AI tools plausibly save architects meaningful time, and the one large visualization survey in this space says most users feel they do. But the specific 60–80% render-time figure, as it circulates in 2026, traces back to unsourced vendor copy and vendor case studies about narrower tasks. Plan against your own timed trial on your own deliverables, and if your bottleneck is somewhere rendering doesn't touch, the headline number won't help you.
