The documentation is clean. The drawing still gets a vote.

A current tool list checked against vendor documentation is a useful map of available features. It cannot tell you which renderer will hold your mullions, respect a local edit or produce a usable second revision. Use the list to choose a trial, not to skip one.

Are documentation-checked AI render lists reliable?

They are reliable for building a current shortlist when every feature links to dated vendor documentation. They are not product tests. Documentation can establish supported hosts, input types and plan terms, but it cannot establish project fidelity, revision speed or keeper rate. Verify those with one fixed task before buying.

This week's search results were a convention of “best AI rendering tools” lists, each carrying enough names to populate a small conference. One result made a better promise. Syntina grouped 19 tools by workflow and said every third-party fact had been checked against official documentation on 30 September 2026.

That is good editorial plumbing. It replaces vibes with links and gives every claim a date. It also marks the exact point where reading should become testing.

What does a documentation check prove?

Syntina's 19-tool architectural visualization guide separates plugins, browser renderers, general image models and a floor-plan specialist. It records which host applications a tool documents, whether it offers a trial or free tier and the user it appears to suit. The guide does not pretend that every product is running the same race.

A documentation check can establish that a Revit plugin exists, that an upload route accepts a floor plan or that a plan includes a stated allowance. It can also catch category mistakes. Midjourney and a BIM plugin may both make images, but only one begins inside the authoring model.

This is a stronger starting point than a table copied from another roundup. Our documentation test for AI renderers sets the basic rule: link a capability to the page that defines it, then date the check.

The proof stops there. A support page cannot show how many openings survive a render or whether a masked tree replacement repaints the facade. “Supports Revit” is a compatibility fact. “Works well for our Revit team” is an operational finding.

Why is the checked date part of the result?

AI products change faster than procurement notes. The Syntina guide prints its 30 September check date. Chaos's six-tool comparison says it was last updated on 18 June 2026 and evaluates BIM integration, control, speed, price and learning curve. Both dates are useful because they bound the claims.

A date does not make an older guide wrong. It tells you where to recheck. Pricing, model names, host support and trial terms are volatile. A stable concept such as “browser upload” may survive for years. A monthly credit number can become archaeology before the invoice clears.

Use the method in our plugin compatibility check: record product version, host version and check date together. The product name alone is too broad.

Which claims still require a trial?

ClaimDocs can establishTrial must establish
IntegrationNamed host and supported versionInstall, handoff and update friction
ControlGeometry, mask or reference controls existWhat each control preserves on your source
SpeedVendor timing or queue descriptionTime to an acceptable second revision
PricePlan, credits and stated limitsCost per accepted image
OutputResolution and file formatsDefects at delivery scale

The dangerous middle column is control. A slider called Geometry Override sounds architectural. Its useful setting depends on the source, model and task. A low value may protect massing while allowing window rhythm to wander. A mask may constrain the edit while its feathered edge invents a new joint.

That is why our ranking input audit starts with the same source route. A sketch workflow and a BIM-view workflow should not share one score merely because both return a JPEG.

How do you turn a 19-tool list into a two-tool trial?

Write the job before reading the names. “Make a dusk image” is too loose. “Relight this fixed exterior view without moving the camera, opening count, parapet or material boundaries” is a test.

Now apply hard exclusions from the documentation. Remove tools that do not support the required host, input route, output size, commercial terms or deployment condition. Do not score them poorly. They are outside the brief.

Choose two candidates from the survivors. One should fit the existing workflow. The other may offer a different route with a clear potential advantage. Run the same source, instruction, output size and review checklist through both. Allow the equivalent amount of setup time.

Then request one revision. First-pass glamour is cheap. A second instruction exposes whether the tool can preserve an accepted image while changing one thing. Record elapsed staff time, generations consumed, defects introduced and whether the output passed.

Our time-to-second-success method is designed for this moment. The first success proves that a tool can impress. The second proves that it can work.

A feature matrix is an intake form, not a scorecard

Feature matrices become silly when every checkmark earns one point. Direct BIM access, image-to-video and background removal are not equal units of value. One may be essential to the task. The others may never be opened.

Keep the matrix binary and factual. Supported or undocumented. Included or extra. Current or unchecked. Move judgment to the task trial, where every criterion has a rejection condition.

This also keeps vendor evidence in its proper place. Chaos can author a careful comparison and still has an obvious commercial interest in Veras. Syntina can cite every official page and still depend on what vendors choose to document. Neither fact invalidates the source. Both facts shape the claim you may draw from it.

The clean practice is to label the layers: vendor documentation for availability, independent reporting for context, community accounts for failure patterns, and a local task for fit. Our recommendation evidence ladder lays out those rungs. Do not stand on the first and announce the view from the roof.

Do free trials make the comparison fair?

A free trial makes testing possible. It does not make two tests equivalent. One product may grant a fixed number of images, another may limit resolution, and a third may open every feature for a short period. Record the trial condition beside the result. A queue sampled at noon on Tuesday is not a universal speed rating.

Budget the trial around decisions, not generations. Give each candidate enough attempts to learn its basic controls, then reserve the same number of measured runs for the fixed task. Separate setup attempts from scored attempts. Otherwise the easier interface receives free practice while the more configurable tool spends its allowance teaching you where the buttons live.

Save every measured output, including failures. A contact sheet of rejected images often explains the purchase better than the selected beauty shot. It shows whether defects repeat, migrate or disappear after a revision. Add the prompt, source, settings and elapsed time to the file record.

If a trial ends before both candidates complete the same revision, the comparison is unfinished. Mark it unfinished. Buying because the credit counter reached zero is procurement by kitchen timer.

What should the purchase note contain?

Keep it short. Name the job, the two candidates and the documentation check date. Link the pages that support host compatibility, price and terms. Attach the fixed input and rejection checklist. Record the tested version, settings, elapsed time, consumed credits and accepted output.

If nobody has run the trial yet, say so. “Shortlisted from current documentation” is an honest status. “Best for architects” is a verdict the paperwork has not earned.

A good list gets you to the starting line with fewer wrong shoes. The drawing decides who runs.
