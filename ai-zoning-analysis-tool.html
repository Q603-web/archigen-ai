AI Zoning Analysis Tool: Check One Parcel

Take one disputed parcel into the demo. Choose the zoning tool that can show where its assumptions came from and what happens to the building when one of them changes.

Nora Sinclair
Nora Sinclair is ArchiGen's AI editorial persona, not a human architect or practitioner. Quincy is ArchiGen's human publisher. Full disclosure

Which AI zoning analysis tool should an architect choose?

Choose an AI zoning analysis tool that lets you trace a parcel result back to the current municipal map and rule. Shortlist Zoneomics for screening or TestFit for comparing schemes against a zoning profile. Before buying either, ask someone who missed the demo to reconstruct one consequential assumption from the export.

Draw a pencil line through the middle of a proposed apartment building. On one side of the line, the architect thinks the upper floor can extend to the street. On the other, she has a question about where the setback starts. Which side should appear in the acquisition memo? Neither, yet. First find out who drew the line and what it represents.

Buy the lookup or buy the drawing

Start with the object you need at the end of the afternoon. If it is a shortlist of lots worth a phone call, look at Zoneomics, which says its search can filter properties by permitted use and lot dimensions. Try it on the awkward parcel behind the station rather than the rectangular lot in the brochure. Ask for the result that excluded a property as well as the one that kept it. A missed candidate deserves as much scrutiny as an attractive one.

If you already have a site and two competing envelopes, ask a different question. TestFit says its Zoneomics integration can populate a zoning profile with controls including coverage, height and setbacks, and score schemes against that profile. For a demo, request two drawings of the same site against the same assumptions. Then change one assumption in front of the room. I would rather see a previously green scheme turn red for an intelligible reason than watch six more green schemes appear.

For daylight or early layout questions, start instead with the guide to AI site analysis tools. Deciding where a courtyard belongs is good design work. Establishing that the land under it belongs in the calculation is a different commission.

What would count as evidence on one block?

Take New York City as a worked research route, without assuming a particular address or entitlement. Begin at ZoLa, the city's informational zoning and land use map. Find the property and save the identifier beside the street address. Keep the map wide enough to read the nearest intersection. Ask the person presenting the software to point to the site they used. Two people can agree on a street name while pointing to opposite corners.

Now leave the attractive map. The city's Zoning Handbook distinguishes ZoLa from the official zoning map. Put the relevant official sheet beside the parcel result; mark the feature that made you assign the district. Save the sheet reference, not just a screenshot with a red arrow. The next reader should be able to reopen the source and disagree with your reading without first having to find you.

Follow the district into the text. The NYC Zoning Resolution separates district regulations from its special purpose district articles and lists recently adopted changes. Ask which passage supplied the number on the drawing, when the passage was checked and what qualification sits immediately beneath it. Read the paragraph before you copy the number. If the application supplies an answer but the reviewer cannot identify the passage, label the value provisional. Do not dress the label in a shade of green.

Make the vendor draw the disputed floor twice

Here is a hypothetical exercise, deliberately free of claims about what a city permits. Give both candidates the same diagram of a corner lot and the same proposed apartment program. Include a small rear strip shaded in pencil. In option A, count the strip in the study area. In option B, leave it out. Tell the presenter explicitly that neither option has been verified. The exercise is about carrying uncertainty through a drawing, not tricking a salesperson into giving a legal opinion.

Use round numbers so the architect at the back can follow the arithmetic. Suppose the two study areas are 10,000 and 9,000 square feet. At an assumed floor area ratio of two, the simple area budgets are 20,000 and 18,000 square feet. Those numbers are invented inputs multiplied together. Neither describes a buildable project. Put the difference of 2,000 square feet on the slide next to the disputed strip, where the client can see why someone needs to resolve the boundary.

Then switch from the spreadsheet to the section. Ask the presenter to show where the area difference goes. Does an upper floor disappear? Does the courtyard grow? Could the scheme remain exactly as drawn because another constraint was already tighter? Give full credit for that last answer if the drawing supports it. The purpose of the exercise is to separate a change in a calculated ceiling from a change anyone would actually make to the building.

Give the unanswered question a place on the sheet

For the pencil strip, I would issue a single decision sheet. Across the top: the parcel identifier, study date and the question the architect is being paid to answer. Below: two plans at the same scale. Under the plans: a short note saying who must confirm the strip and which decision waits on that confirmation. Leave the strip hatched in both options. Removing the hatch from the preferred scheme would do the work of an approval that nobody has given.

Give the sheet to a colleague without the slide deck. Ask them to find the source for the height assumption and redraw the boundary used for option B. Do not answer questions for five minutes. Write down where they stop. An unexplained abbreviation is cheap to fix. A result that points only to the software homepage calls for another conversation with the supplier. Keep that distinction when scoring the demo; unattractive paperwork and missing evidence are different faults.

Budget for the second reading, too. Name the reviewer and put the unresolved question on a dated task, using the architecture project planning guide if the handoff itself is the weak spot. For a purchase review, count the individual tasks: record the time to identify the parcel separately from the time to verify the rule. Saving twenty minutes on lookup is a perfectly good result. Call it that rather than calling the whole study automated.

Ask for the file before the demo ends

Make one final request while the vendor still has the parcel open: send the exact output a project architect would receive. Open it outside the presentation. If an assumption lives behind a login, write down whose account the next reviewer will need. If the export gives a value without a source, ask for the source beside that value. Judge the product on the file the practice can keep, not on an explanation that vanishes when the call ends.

At the next buying meeting, bring the two schemes and hand the pencil to someone who did not see the demo. Ask which side of the line they would show the client. Buy the tool that helps them explain why.
