“Free” is a label. Find the contract.

A free AI rendering plan may be a demo, a personal-use allowance or a useful production lane. The price column cannot tell you which. Check who qualifies, what projects are allowed, what leaves the platform and what happens when the meter or account closes.

Can architects use a free AI renderer for client work?

Only when the current plan permits the account type, project use and commercial output, while providing acceptable resolution, privacy and export rights. Read the plan page and governing terms together. Save a dated copy. “Free tier” describes price, not permission, production fitness or ownership.

A comparison table surfaced this morning with a column labelled “free tier.” Useful instinct. Terrible stopping point. One green tick can mean a handful of trial images, a continuing personal account, watermarked output, low-resolution exports or a plan barred from commercial work.

Those offers can all cost zero at checkout. They are not the same product.

Architecture makes the distinction expensive. The input may show an unbuilt client project. The output may enter a fee proposal, planning presentation or competition board. A practice needs permission and custody, not merely a button that works without a card.

What does “free” fail to answer?

The label says who pays today. It says nothing about who may open the account, which projects may enter, how many useful outputs can leave or whether the files remain accessible after the allowance ends.

Roundups flatten these fields because a compact table is pleasant to scan. Procurement should expand them again. Our pricing-unit audit makes the same point about subscriptions, credits and generations: unlike units do not become comparable when placed in one column.

Start with the person holding the account

A free plan may be limited to individuals, students, educators or noncommercial users. An employee creating an account with a work email does not automatically turn an individual permission into a company licence. Nor does a student entitlement follow somebody into paid practice because the same laptop is involved.

Name the account owner and intended operators. Check whether credentials may be shared, whether team members need separate seats and whether generated work can be transferred to the firm. If the only practical method is a common login passed around the studio, the plan has already failed an ordinary access-control test.

The distinction matters when somebody leaves. A project archive tied to one person's free account is a future scavenger hunt. The workflow bus-factor test applies to subscriptions too: the practice must be able to recover source, settings and outputs without its resident enthusiast.

Does the project qualify?

“Commercial use allowed” and “free to use” are separate claims. Check the terms that govern the exact plan, not a search snippet or an old review. Look for restrictions on client work, paid services, competitions, advertising and resale. If different documents conflict, pause the use and ask the vendor in writing.

Permission to use an output does not settle every right around it. Inputs may contain client confidential information, licensed photography, branded furniture or artwork. Outputs can inherit recognizable material even when a platform grants broad commercial terms. ArchiGen's commercial-license guide separates the model licence, service terms and copyright question because a single “commercial” badge cannot carry all three.

Save the plan name, terms URL and access date with the project. Terms change. A dated record does not freeze the contract, but it gives the office evidence of what it reviewed when the decision was made.

Can the free output survive delivery?

Open the exported file before admiring the gallery preview. Record pixel dimensions, file format, compression, watermark, metadata and any attribution requirement. A generous generation allowance can still produce files unsuitable for print or post-production.

Check whether the plan permits upscaling, transparent backgrounds, masks or lossless downloads. Ask whether an export can be re-downloaded after the credit balance reaches zero. The useful product is the file that enters the next step, not the thumbnail sitting inside the service.

Do one revision. If an approved view needs a material correction, can the free plan recall the source and settings, or does it force a fresh image? Cheap generation becomes costly when each change abandons the previous result.

Our output-package test ranks alpha, masks, depth and bit depth beside the beauty image. A free plan does not need every production pass. It does need to state which ones are missing.

Count useful work, not free presses

A daily or monthly allowance is a meter. Determine whether previews, failed jobs, variations, edits, upscales and video consume the same unit. Check whether unused credits roll over and whether the vendor can change the rate by model or resolution.

Then budget an evaluation before pressing buttons. The trial-credit plan reserves runs for repeatability, a change request and a small image set. That is more revealing than spending the whole allowance on attractive first passes.

A continuing free plan may still be a fine learning lane. Keep it out of the production schedule until its refill rate can support revision. A deadline does not care that next month's credits are technically free.

Where do the files go when the plan ends?

Before uploading client material, identify retention, deletion and training choices. Check whether the free plan receives different privacy controls from paid accounts. A feature available on the enterprise page should not be assumed at zero cost.

Export source references, prompts, settings, seeds where available, outputs and an account activity record. Test deletion on a harmless sample. The client-confidentiality review explains why upload permission belongs in project governance, not in an operator's personal judgement at midnight.

Also plan the conversion. If the office upgrades, does the same account retain its history? If it leaves, is there a bulk export? If the service closes free access, can the practice reproduce the approved image elsewhere? A free lane that traps the archive is charging in a different currency.

How should comparison tables describe free plans?

Replace the tick with a short status: trial allowance, continuing personal plan, education plan or commercial free plan. Add the allowance unit, export ceiling and date checked. Link the plan source. Mark unknowns as unknown.

That sentence will be longer than “yes.” It will also be useful.

The Syntina roundup surfaced today says its tool details were checked against vendors' own documents and includes a free-tier field. That is a better starting posture than copying price snippets. Readers still need the underlying plan date and the five checks above before moving a candidate into studio work.

ArchiGen's take: zero price needs more reading

Free access is valuable. It lets a practice learn the interface and reject a poor fit before procurement begins. It can support students, experiments and low-volume work. None of that turns the label into a licence.

Treat every free plan as a small contract with an unusual invoice. Name the user. Classify the project. Inspect the output, meter and exit. If those answers fit, use it with confidence.

The button costs nothing. The ambiguity does.
