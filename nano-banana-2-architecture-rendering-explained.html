Nano Banana 2, explained for architects.

Nano Banana 2 is Google DeepMind's Gemini 3.1 Flash Image, launched on 26 February 2026 and now succeeded by Nano Banana 2.1. Google calls it native Gemini image generation and has never described it as a diffusion model. Architects reach it through Gemini, Google's API, ComfyUI, Veras, Rendair and Fenestra.

Is Nano Banana 2 a diffusion model?

Google has not said so. Its developer docs call Nano Banana "Gemini's native image generation capabilities," and the model card says Gemini 3.1 Flash Image "is based on Gemini 3 Flash," which traces back to a sparse mixture-of-experts transformer. Google has not published how the pixels are decoded, so "diffusion" is a guess.

The first version of this page got the basics wrong. It called Nano Banana 2 a Chaos engine trained on matched clay-and-photo pairs, said it was running half the year's architectural renders, and reported two weeks of tests on three live projects. The model is Google's. The market share was invented. The tests never happened. What follows is rebuilt from Google's documentation, vendor release notes and two tests ArchiGen did run and published separately.

What is Nano Banana 2, and which one are you using?

Google launched it on 26 February 2026 as "Nano Banana 2 (Gemini 3.1 Flash Image), our latest state-of-the-art image model," pitched in the launch post as Nano Banana Pro's quality "at lightning-fast speed." The Gemini API changelog moved it to general availability on 28 May 2026, added a Lite version on 30 June, and on 6 October released Nano Banana 2.1 as its update. Google's image-generation docs now call Nano Banana 2 "the previous-generation high-efficiency workhorse model" and recommend 2.1 for new projects. Five models share the name:

What does Google say about the model architecture?

Less than the search results imply. The Nano Banana 2 model card says the model "is based on Gemini 3 Flash" and belongs to "a suite of highly-capable, natively multimodal reasoning models." The Gemini 3 Flash card says that model "is based on Gemini 3 Pro," and the Gemini 3 Pro card describes a "sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE)" transformer with native multimodal input. None of those Google documents uses the word diffusion, and none explains how image output is produced. Images are billed as output tokens, 1,120 of them for a 1K image per the pricing page, but billing units are not an architecture diagram.

Outside analysis goes further. Developer Max Woolf, writing about the original Nano Banana in November 2025, concluded that "it is indeed autoregressive, generating 1,290 tokens per image." That is one informed reading of an earlier model, not a Google statement about Nano Banana 2.

For architects the label matters less than the documented behaviour. The model card lists its own known limits: text rendering "poor in small text (often blurry in 1k model)," "occasional confusion around spatial localisation (e.g. left/right etc.)," and "still limited in advanced capabilities with world knowledge, 3D reasoning and factuality." Left and right, and 3D reasoning. For a building image, those are the two that bite.

Where can architects use Nano Banana 2?

Google's own surfaces. At launch, Google said Nano Banana 2 would replace Nano Banana Pro in the Gemini app and become the default image model in Flow, with preview access in AI Studio, the Gemini API and Vertex AI. Google's developer post notes that "a paid api key is required to use the model on Google AI Studio." The model card's distribution list names only Google-hosted products and APIs; there are no downloadable weights.

ComfyUI. Comfy added it the same day through Partner Nodes, which its docs describe as "calling closed-source models through API requests" on a prepaid credit system. The graph runs locally; the image is made on Google's servers. Our piece on Gemini for the concept, ComfyUI for the render covers where that split makes sense.

Chaos Veras. Veras uses Google's models; it does not make or license them out. Veras 4.0 introduced "Render Engine 7, powered by Nano Banana Pro," and Veras 4.2, published on 6 March 2026, added Nano Banana 2 with "more quota and faster render times with similar quality to Nano Banana Pro." The old claim that Veras 4.3 brought the engine "for free" with a V-Ray licence is wrong twice. As listed on 28 October 2026, the Veras page sells Starter at $16.90 a month billed annually ($202.80), "Up to 50~ Nano Banana 2 1K images monthly," and Pro at $29.00 a month billed annually ($348). Our Veras review tracks the releases since.

Rendair and Fenestra. Rendair announced that "Google's Nano Banana 2 is now integrated into Rendair Chat" on 27 February 2026; Fenestra claimed "day-zero access" on launch day. The two take different routes to models, set out in Veras against Rendair.

Which specs matter for architectural images?

Per Google's image-generation docs, Nano Banana 2 outputs at 1K, 2K and 4K and adds a 512px tier that 2.1 drops. Its aspect ratios run from 1:1 to the new 4:1, 1:4, 8:1 and 1:8, which suits long street elevations and tall section strips better than the usual square. The docs say you can "mix up to 14 reference images," which is how a massing view, a material board and a site photo end up in one prompt. "All generated images include a SynthID watermark," and Google adds C2PA Content Credentials.

Text is the weak spot Google admits to. With small type "often blurry in 1k model," per the model card, signage and title blocks deserve a 2K pass or a check by eye before anything leaves the office.

What did ArchiGen's documented tests show?

Two, both small, both published with their method. In the floor-plan comparison of 4 May 2026, one anonymised residential plan went through Nano Banana 2 and GPT Image 2, "two variants per model, four renders total." The page states that this was "not a single-variable test": model, edit endpoint and prompt format all changed together. Nano Banana 2 "preserved layout, room count and aspect ratio, at about 37 seconds per variant," at $0.04 a piece as that run recorded it; GPT Image 2 gave stronger mood but squared off the footprint and added rooms.

The signage bench of 25 July 2026 ran "5 paired first-generations per model, identical prompt and production pipeline config, one signage-heavy scene, total spend $0.37." Nano Banana 2 Lite's median time was 2.52 seconds against 5.41 for Nano Banana 2, at $0.0342 against $0.0387 per image. Both set legible signage in five of five takes; Nano Banana 2 was "steadier" on exact glyphs. The bench calls its own sample "still small," and so should anyone quoting it. The full plan workflow sits in our Nano Banana floor-plan guide.

Should you build a workflow on Nano Banana 2 now?

Build on the family, not the version. Google has already deprecated the model this page is named after, its successor costs half as much per 1K image, and Veras's pricing page still counts its credits in "Nano Banana 2" images. That gap will close quietly, inside someone else's product, without a release note you will read. Keep the source drawing next to every output and check the geometry yourself, because the model card already told you what it struggles with.

The banana is a brand. The drawing is still yours to check.
