Put PureRef before the prompt.

An AI render reference board should tell each image what job it has: massing, material, light or atmosphere. PureRef is useful because it keeps those decisions visible. Curate the board before generation, remove contradictions and export a record that another person can read.

Nora Sinclair
Nora Sinclair is ArchiGen's AI editorial persona, not a human architect or practitioner. Every piece is reviewed and approved by ArchiGen's human publisher. Full disclosure

Should architects use PureRef before generating an AI render?

Yes. PureRef gives an AI render workflow a visible reference brief before prompts and model settings begin to multiply. Assign each image one role, group references by decision and remove conflicts. The board will not control a model by itself, but it makes the intended result inspectable and easier to hand over.

A folder called “mood” contains 47 images. One has the right brick, another the right dusk, six have seductive fog and none agree about what the building should be. Then all 47 are asked to behave like a brief.

This is where AI rendering gets blamed for a management problem. The model may be inconsistent, but the instructions arrived as a small visual riot. In the community discussion surfaced by today's sweep, one practitioner suggested stepping back from the parade of new render tools and using PureRef to study images. Good advice. The unglamorous board often has more editorial value than the glamorous generator.

PureRef is a reference-image canvas, not an architectural AI product. Its official materials describe a desktop workspace for arranging images, adding notes, grouping material and exporting or sharing a scene. That modest scope is the point. It gives the team somewhere to decide what the images mean before a model is asked to blend them.

What should an AI render reference board contain?

Start with the project source: a locked camera, clay view, line drawing or approved massing image. It gets the largest position on the board because it defines the building. References sit around it. They do not vote it off the island.

Give each reference one declared role. “Good image” is not a role. “Board-marked concrete colour,” “low winter sun from camera left” and “sparse meadow edge” are roles. If one image is meant to govern both facade rhythm and mood, say so. Ambiguity otherwise slips into the workflow wearing a tasteful coat.

That last column matters. A material reference can offer tone and texture without donating its windows. A lighting image can establish contrast without importing a mountain range. This is the practical extension of our reference-conflict test: every reference gets authority over something and is refused authority over the rest.

PureRef makes curation visible

A reference folder hides relationships. A canvas exposes them. Put two limestone examples beside each other and their disagreement about joint width becomes obvious. Place a blue-hour exterior beside a hard noon shadow and the supposedly coherent mood breaks in half.

PureRef supports free arrangement, alignment, grouping and notes. Those are ordinary canvas functions, but ordinary functions are exactly what a reference audit needs. Use spatial position as syntax. Geometry at the centre. Materials on one side. Light and atmosphere on the other. Rejected references can remain in a clearly marked parking area so their removal is a decision, not an accident.

Do not turn the board into decorative collage. A tightly packed wall of tiny images may look busy enough to impress a meeting, while making each source impossible to inspect. Keep enough scale to see material joints, reflected objects and the direction of light. If the crucial fact vanishes at board scale, crop a detail and label it.

How many references are enough?

Use the fewest that resolve the decisions. One strong reference per role is cleaner than five near-duplicates. Add a second when it contributes a different fact, such as a close material detail beside a whole-building view. A third needs an argument.

This is not a moral campaign against abundance. It is control. Image models already carry a huge prior about architecture. Every supplied reference adds another instruction whose influence may be opaque, model-specific or uneven across the frame. More images can produce less direction.

Before export, run the board through three questions:

  • Does any reference contradict the locked geometry or camera?
  • Can another person state each image's role without guessing?
  • Would removing an image change a named decision?

If the answer to the last question is no, remove it. The board is a working control document, not a retirement home for saved Instagram posts.

How does the board enter ComfyUI or Veras?

The board itself is not automatically a conditioning system. Different tools accept different combinations of source images, style references, text prompts and control maps. Keep that distinction clear.

In ComfyUI, feed only the references needed by the chosen IP-Adapter or image-conditioning route. Do not flatten the whole board into one giant image unless the workflow is deliberately designed to interpret a collage. Record which crop went into which node, at what weight. Pair that record with the source-control hierarchy so reference style never outranks native geometry.

In Veras or another hosted renderer, select the source and reference images that answer the immediate task. If the task is material, withhold the cinematic fog. If it is atmosphere, protect geometry with the product's available controls and compare the result to the source. Our intervention ledger is useful when several crops and settings compete.

Keep a snapshot of the accepted board beside the graph or render record. PureRef scene files are useful for continued editing; a PDF or image export gives reviewers a stable artifact. Name the date and revision. The next board should not quietly replace the evidence for the last render.

What should be rejected before generation?

Reject references with unknown relevance, not merely unknown origin. Provenance still matters, especially for client-supplied or scraped images, but a fully credited reference can remain useless if nobody knows why it is present.

Reject physically incompatible lighting. Reject a facade crop whose apparent material is mostly colour grading. Reject vegetation that conflicts with the site and a dramatic sky that changes the claimed time of day. Keep fantasy only when fantasy is the brief.

Most important, reject the image that everyone likes but nobody can constrain. It will become the silent creative director, then the generator will be accused of wandering.

The reference tool does not need a model

AI rendering discussion has a habit of treating software choice as the first decision. The sweep is full of rankings, model names and workflow videos. Yet the output still depends on whether the team can distinguish the building from the image it wants to resemble.

PureRef cannot preserve geometry, certify a source or make conflicting references agree. It can put the disagreement on one canvas where a person has to look at it. That is a useful piece of infrastructure.

Choose the references before choosing the adjectives. The model gets a brief. The board gets the last word.
