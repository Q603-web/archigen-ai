Rendershop AI, read closely.

Rendershop AI is worth a cheap trial if you want fast concept renders from sketches or model screenshots: it is a browser tool from Two Cases, LLC, with plans from $9 to $119 a month. Read its terms before uploading client work, because it takes a broad licence over inputs and outputs. ArchiGen has not run the tool.

What is Rendershop AI, and is it worth trying?

Rendershop (rendershop.ai) is a browser-based AI rendering studio for architects and designers. You upload an image of a sketch, model view or CAD sheet, choose style and lighting, and it returns a photorealistic render. Paid plans run $9 to $119 a month on credits. Free credits make a trial cheap; read the terms before uploading client work.

The first thing on rendershop.ai is not a render. It is a control panel: Architectural Style set to Modern, a Scale Factor of 2x, 4x or 10x, a prompt box pre-filled with "modern exterior, warm stone cladding, daytime...", and three lighting buttons labelled Sunrise, Noon and Night. The pitch is fewer decisions, made before a word of copy.

This is a documentation review, not a test. ArchiGen has not run Rendershop. What follows comes from Rendershop's own pages and terms, read on 10 October 2026, plus the little outside coverage that exists. Vendor statements are labelled as such.

Six tools behind one upload box

Rendershop is made by Two Cases, LLC, according to its terms of service, and calls itself "The All-in-One AI Rendering Platform for Design." The homepage lists six tools. Render turns a sketch, elevation or model screenshot into a photoreal image. Edit and Photo Edit change parts of a render or a real photograph. Enhance upscales. Staging furnishes an empty room photo. Video animates a still into a short camera move.

The Render documentation walks through the settings: a scene type (Exterior, Interior, Landscape or Floor Plan), resolution at 1K for drafts, 2K standard or 4K, architectural and render styles from Photorealistic to Watercolor, lighting, camera angle, weather and optional reference images. The prompt is optional. The docs promise that "The AI keeps the geometry of your input and adds the materials, lighting, and context." It is the claim to check first.

Edit is the more interesting tool for practice. The homepage describes markup points you click onto a surface, drawn regions for broad changes, an eraser for objects, and reference photos for a specific finish, with "everything else stays untouched." That is inpainting with an architect-friendly face on it, and how inpainting works and where it fails applies here as it does anywhere.

A picture in, a picture out

The most useful sentence on the whole site sits in the Supported inputs page: "Rendershop works from a flat image, not a BIM model." It continues: "There is no plugin and no file-format requirement, because the input is a picture." A SketchUp, Revit, Rhino, Blender or 3ds Max screenshot counts, as does a PDF elevation or a photographed sketch.

Credit to Rendershop for saying it plainly. The trade is real, though. A tool that only sees pixels cannot read depth, layers or material IDs from your model, so anything it gets right about the geometry, it got right by reading a picture. That is the core difference between a browser renderer and a plugin like Chaos Veras, set out in Veras versus Rendair and, more generally, in native BIM integration versus PNG upload. The upside, per the getting-started page: "there is nothing to install and no GPU required."

How much does Rendershop cost?

Usage is credit-based, and every tool draws on one monthly balance. The credits and billing page prices a Render, Edit, Photo Edit or Staging job at 10 credits, an Enhance at 1 credit and video at 5 credits per second. The plans, from that page and the pricing page:

The last column is simple division by ArchiGen, not a Rendershop figure. Prices are per user, every plan gets the same tools and up to 4K output, and the free tier needs no card. The page for firms says "50 free credits" and mentions custom enterprise pricing for high volume. One small oddity: the homepage still says "then from $19/mo" while the pricing page lists a $9 Starter plan.

Two details change the arithmetic. Unused credits "do not roll over between billing cycles," per the terms, and a ten-second video costs 50 credits, the same as five renders. The headline price per generation is also not the price per image you would show a client; cost per usable image explains why that gap decides the real bill.

Seconds, minutes, or unlimited?

Rendershop's pages disagree on speed. The homepage headline says "Render your projects in seconds." The firms page advertises "30 sec Average Render Time" and "Unlimited Revisions." The FAQ, more soberly, says "Most renders are generated within 2-5 minutes, depending on the complexity and resolution." Revisions are not unlimited in any billing sense, either: each render costs 10 credits from a capped monthly pool. Even video length drifts: 4 to 10 seconds at the top of the video docs, 2 to 10 seconds a few lines further down, and 4 to 12 seconds on the connector page.

None of this is sinister; the marketing and the documentation were simply written in different moods. The firms page also claims "<$1 Per Render vs $500-2000 traditional." The first half matches the pricing table. The second half compares a concept image to a commissioned visualization, which is a bit like comparing a napkin sketch to a set of drawings by weight.

Who owns a Rendershop render?

You do, with conditions. The FAQ says "all renders generated on Rendershop are yours to use commercially," including client presentations and marketing. The terms (last updated 17 March 2026) say you own the Assets "to the fullest extent permitted by applicable law," a phrase doing real work while copyright in AI output stays unsettled; who owns an AI render covers the layers.

The same section grants Rendershop "a perpetual, worldwide, non-exclusive, sublicensable, royalty-free, irrevocable license" over both your inputs and your outputs, for operating, promoting and improving the service. "This includes using data to train and refine our rendering algorithms, subject to any opt-out features provided in your account settings." The privacy policy adds "Where available" to that opt-out and says uploads may be processed on third-party cloud GPU clusters. In fairness, a confidentiality clause promises inputs will not be showcased without written consent. For an unbuilt client scheme, find the opt-out first; cloud rendering and client confidentiality lists what to check.

Then there are refunds. The billing docs say credits are "returned automatically if it fails or is rejected, so a failure never costs you anything." The terms say credits consumed are "final and non-refundable, regardless of whether the operation completes successfully or fails." The terms are the contract. They also label every output "FOR VISUALIZATION AND CONCEPTUAL PURPOSES ONLY" and send disputes to arbitration in Wayne County, Michigan.

The renderer that lives in a chat window

The newest part of the product is an MCP connector that plugs Rendershop into Claude and ChatGPT. It lists twelve tools, from render_image to generate_video, billed at studio credit rates, and says videos need the user's confirmation of price first, generations are rate-limited per account, and upload links expire after 15 minutes.

The copy says "Brief it like you'd brief a visualizer." The rendering interface is becoming a conversation, and the visualizer being briefed is a model. Whether that is good news depends on who used to take the brief.

Who is writing the Rendershop reviews?

Mostly Rendershop. Its blog post "10 AI Architecture Tools Every Architect Needs To Know in 2026," signed "The Rendershop Team" and dated 10 February 2026, puts Rendershop at number one. The site also publishes competitor pages such as "Renderahouse Review 2026: Features, Pricing & Better Alternative," which scores the rival 3.4 out of 5 and recommends Rendershop instead. That is ordinary search marketing. It is not a review.

The nearest thing to outside coverage is a Norton Research comparison dated 3 February 2026, which ranks Rendershop first at 95 out of 100 and states that it draws on "publicly available descriptions of service offerings, pricing models, and feature lists from vendor websites," naming rendershop.ai among them. In other words, it scored the brochure. ArchiGen's searches on 10 October 2026 turned up no independent hands-on review. Is Rendershop worth trying?

For the price of a sign-up, yes, with eyes open. On its own description, Rendershop suits concept images from screenshots and sketches, material swaps and staging empty-room photos, for the students, design firms and custom home builders its use-case pages name, and for anyone who wants nothing to install. It is not a route to images that must match a BIM model line for line, and its terms say as much.

Spend the free credits on your hardest view: the cantilever, the odd roof. Run an Edit and see whether "everything else stays untouched" holds. Then confirm the training opt-out exists before anything confidential goes in.

The trial will tell you whether the pictures are good. The terms tell you what you signed.
