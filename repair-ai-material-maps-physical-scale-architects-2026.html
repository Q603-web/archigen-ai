Repair AI material maps without losing scale.

A material generator can recover plausible color, roughness, normal and height maps from a tired sample. Plausible is not measured. Keep one known dimension through cleanup, generation, tiling and export, then judge the material on a full wall under two lights.

Nora Sinclair

How should architects repair AI-generated material maps?

Preserve a known real dimension in the source, correct perspective and lighting before map generation, keep every channel registered, and tile the full material set together. Export physical-size metadata where supported. Approve the result only after checking repetition, relief and reflectance on a correctly scaled scene surface.

A user in this week's archviz discussion describes one of AI's quieter useful jobs: making material maps from a poor sample. No cinematic villa. No prompt about golden-hour serenity. Just a low-quality swatch that needs to become workable.

This is exactly where generative assistance earns its chair. It is also where a convincing preview can conceal a basic error. The timber grain looks crisp, the normal map has bite, and every board has quietly grown to forty centimeters wide.

The repair needs a scale witness: one feature whose real dimension survives every stage. Without it, the software can improve pixels while destroying the material.

What should the source image preserve?

Keep the largest front-facing patch available. Avoid a crop dominated by a single knot, stain or chipped corner; once tiled, that memorable feature will march down the facade like a logo. Correct lens perspective before asking a model to infer channels.

Include one measured span. It might be a brick length, tile width, board module or the distance between two aggregate features recorded at capture. A ruler in the raw photograph is useful, but crop it out only after its pixels have established the physical width.

Save the untouched source beside the working crop. A screenshot copied from a supplier PDF may be the only input available, yet its color can contain page compression, studio lighting and display transforms. Name that uncertainty. AI does not turn borrowed pixels into laboratory measurement.

Why should cleanup happen before map generation?

A bright patch in the source may be illumination, a pale aggregate or polished wear. If it remains unresolved, the generator must guess. Its base color may bake in the highlight while its roughness map also makes the same area glossy. The result responds to new light twice.

Adobe's Image to Material documentation says the AI-powered method generates normal, height and roughness maps and attempts to remove shadows and highlights from albedo. That is a vendor-described capability, not permission to skip source correction.

Crop, straighten, equalize broad illumination and remove obvious non-material debris first. Adobe's own tools guidance recommends correcting the image before conversion because a single image channel is simpler to repair than several generated channels. Sensible software advice, and refreshingly short of fireworks.

How does the scale witness survive generation?

Record the working crop in physical units and pixels. If 1,600 pixels represent 800 millimeters, keep that relationship with the source notes. Generation may change resolution, but the exported material should still cover the same 800-millimeter span unless a deliberate crop changes it.

Substance 3D Sampler's physical-size workflow supports manual calibration between two features or an estimate from scan metadata. Adobe says the physical size can travel in an SBSAR file and influence image export proportions. Manual measurement is the safer route for phone photographs and screenshots; DPI metadata is only meaningful for a calibrated scan.

Keep the dimension in the asset name or sidecar record as well. Metadata can disappear when maps are exported, renamed or moved through another renderer. “Oak boards, 800 mm sample width” survives a file browser better than “wood_final_03.”

Which channels need separate suspicion?

Base color should describe surface color without cast shadow or bright reflection. Roughness should control how broadly light reflects, not serve as a grayscale copy of the photograph. Normal and height should agree about relief direction and relative strength.

Metalness deserves particular restraint. Brick, stone, timber and plaster are not partly metallic because the generator found a bright fleck. Set nonmetal materials accordingly unless the source genuinely combines metal with another surface.

Inspect each map alone, then render the set. Channel coherence matters more than individual drama. A handsome normal map paired with an unrelated roughness map produces a surface that sparkles in one light and turns to porridge in another.

Our photo-to-PBR guide covers capture and channel roles. The narrower point here is repair: do not accept a replacement map merely because its thumbnail contains more detail. Check whether the detail occurs in the same place across the set.

When should the material become tileable?

Early enough that later filters do not preserve a bad seam across every channel. Adobe's Tiling filter documentation describes scaling and offsetting the material to create overlap, then varying the overlap edge. It also warns that scaling can cost detail.

Tile all channels through a material-aware process. Repairing only base color can leave a seam in normal or roughness that appears under raking light. If a manual clone is unavoidable, repeat it consistently or regenerate dependent maps from the repaired source.

Preview a grid of at least four by four tiles. A boundary can disappear while a distinctive central knot repeats with comic precision. A hidden boundary and a quiet repeat are different achievements. Our reference-scale audit applies the same discipline to generated material proposals.

How should the repaired material be tested in a scene?

Apply it to a surface with known dimensions and a camera that shows enough area to reveal repetition. Test the intended close view and a wider view. A material can survive a thumbnail while failing across twelve meters of facade.

Use two lighting conditions. A broad soft light reveals color and repetition. A low raking light exposes normal direction, height exaggeration and seams. Keep displacement modest until the map proves it can describe joints without chewing the silhouette.

Compare the render with the source sample at equivalent scale, not equivalent screen size. If the sample contains three bricks across 600 millimeters, the scene should show that count across 600 modeled millimeters. The eye is generous with attractive textures. Dimensions are less sociable.

Then inspect transitions at corners, openings and material boundaries. A texture that reads well on an infinite plane may reveal poor UV orientation at a return. The material-study workflow is useful for design options, but a production material must survive actual geometry.

What should be saved with the final maps?

Keep the source image, measured span, crop dimensions, correction steps, generator and version, material model, channel list, tile extent and export resolution. Store one neutral sphere or wall preview and one scene-scale proof.

If the tool generated missing information, say so. Roughness inferred from a photograph is an interpretation. Height inferred from color is an interpretation. Useful does not mean measured.

Version the complete set together. Replacing a base-color map without checking the derived roughness and normal maps creates a family resemblance problem: related filenames, different material.

Our take: better pixels still need a ruler

AI map repair can rescue a bad sample and save dull manual work. The risk is not flamboyant invention. It is a material that looks credible while being physically anonymous.

Keep one dimension alive from source to wall. The ruler is the least glamorous node in the graph, which is why it has the best chance of saving the render.
