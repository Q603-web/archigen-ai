Is TestFit worth it for architects and developers in 2026?

For US developer-side feasibility on multifamily, industrial, retail or parking-led sites, yes, it is worth a demo: TestFit generates site plans, unit mixes, parking and pro forma yields together. Site Solver starts at $15,000 a year, Parking Solver at $195 a month. For environmental analysis or Autodesk-first teams, Forma Site Design fits better.

The most revealing line TestFit has published this year is a release headline. TestFit 5.25 "moves the question from what fits to what earns." That is a company telling you who its customer is. Not the architect wondering whether the courtyard gets sun. The developer wondering whether the deal clears its yield on cost before anyone draws a stair.

This is a documentation review, not a test: ArchiGen has not run TestFit on any site. Every claim below comes from a page opened on 10 October 2026 and is labelled by who made it.

What TestFit says it does

TestFit sells two products. Site Solver is the feasibility engine. You pick a site from parcel data, an APN or metes and bounds, build a zoning profile from FAR, dwelling units per acre and parking ratio, set rules for stairs, firewalls and elevators, and let it place cores or place them yourself. It balances cut and fill, generates options "within seconds", and sorts them by FAR, parking ratio or yield on cost. Presets take your own unit mix and sizes, "down to balcony size." The typology list on TestFit's homepage runs multifamily, single-family, industrial, data centers, retail, hotel and parking.

Parking Solver is the narrower tool (TestFit's MCP explainer calls it self-serve): surface and structured layouts generated from parcel size and shape, target ratios tracked in real time, and ADA, EV, compact and trailer stalls editable by hand. Around both sit add-ons. Site Intelligence layers in zoning data, FEMA flood maps, US wetlands, SSURGO soil data, utilities and 3D terrain with cut and fill. Pro Forma imports your existing Excel model and live-links its cells to model quantities, so the spreadsheet moves when the scheme does.

Notice that last one. TestFit's pitch is that the pro forma no longer arrives separately from the test fit: it sits in the same window.

Where is the AI in TestFit?

In two places, and TestFit is unusually candid about which one does the work. The first is generative design. AEC Magazine reported its July 2024 launch plans, with chief executive Clifton Harness saying "a machine can test every possible configuration, inclusive of what we would never even think to try." Release 5.19 made generative design run in the background as you lay out a site, and added deal creation in the browser. Release 5.25 attached a full pro forma to every housing solve in a generative set, so you can filter by IRR, yield, cost or revenue and let the losers drop out.

The second is the MCP connection, which a TestFit press release dated 28 July 2026 said would roll out on 5 August. TestFit's own explainer draws the line hard: "The assistant only directs. It never generates the geometry, the site configuration, or the numbers behind it." The MCP product page adds that TestFit "has no model to train" because the product runs on deterministic algorithms rather than an LLM. You bring your own assistant, and the connector runs through the desktop app.

Much of design software in 2026 is racing to let a language model draw. TestFit has made the chatbot a receptionist and kept the solver as the professional. Whether "no hallucinations, only buildable outcomes", the vendor's phrase, holds on a messy parcel is exactly what none of the independent sources reviewed here has tested.

What does TestFit cost?

TestFit publishes its prices. Its pricing page lists Parking Solver at $195 a month billed monthly, with unlimited users, and three add-ons: Site Intelligence at $150 a month, Pro Forma at $170 and the MCP Connection at $100. Site Solver starts at $15,000 a year and adds generative design, custom unit types, the MCP connection and an account manager. Site Solver Portfolio starts at $20,000 a year with enterprise SSO. As recently as 11 August 2026, a comparison by Hektar, a competitor that says openly its post "is not neutral", described TestFit pricing as unpublished. Feasibility software moves faster than the blog posts about it.

Read the split carefully. The $195 tier is a parking and yield tool. The generative engine most people mean when they search "TestFit AI" sits behind the five-figure annual Site Solver contract.

Getting a TestFit scheme into Revit

TestFit's integrations page lists a Revit add-in that imports sites and buildings, .dxf export for AutoCAD with geolocation, .skp for SketchUp, and general exports to .csv, .glTF and .pdf. The detail of what survives the trip, which elements and which parameters, sits in TestFit's support knowledge base, and that asked for a sign-in when opened. Judging the handoff needs a demo, not a datasheet. For what "integration" should mean, see our explainer on what BIM integration means and the BIM-first AI stack for Revit and Archicad firms.

Autodesk's product details page lists wind and noise in a "rapid" mode built on machine learning predictions. Price is the blunt difference: Forma's annual standalone price is under five percent of TestFit's entry Site Solver figure, and firms already on the AEC Collection get it in the bundle. Hektar's comparison reaches the same split, calling Forma's analysis suite "the deepest" in the category and TestFit "strong on construction cost and constructability, less developed on environmental analysis."

So "Forma or TestFit" is really two different questions. Forma asks whether the massing performs on its site. TestFit asks whether the deal pencils. A Revit practice that needs daylight and wind evidence for a planning board starts with Forma. A developer, broker or developer-side architect running parcel after parcel through yield screens is TestFit's stated customer. Would professionals recommend TestFit?

The public evidence is thinner than the marketing. TestFit's page for architects cites a 2026 survey of its own customers: 78% of architects say it increases feasibility accuracy, and 92% say they win more work by offering more design options within a fixed fee. Those are vendor figures. TestFit's own usage numbers also disagree on scale: the architects and pro forma pages say 650+ sites or deals a week, while the July press release says over 3,200 deals a week.

Independent coverage is mostly news. Dallas Innovates reported on 2 September 2026 that the company was founded in 2016 and has raised $22 million, including a $20 million Series A in 2022 led by Parkway Venture Capital. G2 and Capterra both list TestFit, but both blocked automated access when checked, so this article does not quote their ratings or reviews.

What the documents do support is a clear profile. TestFit is a specialist deal-screening engine, heavy on US data layers, with a well-defined solver and a growing financial layer, and its AI story is carefully fenced. That is a recommendation for the right buyer and a pass for everyone else.

Ask TestFit what fits, and it will tell you. Ask it what is beautiful, and it will send you to Revit.
